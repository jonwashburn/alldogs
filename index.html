ALL DOGS™

  

1,000 dogs · one zombie apocalypse · adoption fee $50 · one per person

ALL DOGS is a year-long performance played by a thousand strangers holding one dog each. The artwork is whatever is left when the year ends. Every dog is its own drawing living on Ethereum: the artist draws the dogs, the owners write the story.

The game

Zombie apocalypse

A thousand people each adopt one dog and keep it for a year. A dog you keep grows older every day and rarer every week. Selling it kills it: for that whole year, the moment a living dog changes hands the contract turns it into a zombie. If 210 dogs are sold inside the year, the apocalypse comes and every dog still living turns with them.

1

Start by adopting your dog. $50 at the gate, one dog per confirmed handle. Your handle goes on the collar.

2

Then decide: keep it, or sell it. Selling is the kill. A living dog that changes hands turns in the same transaction, so the buyer receives a corpse and nobody can ever buy a living dog. Your sale is one of the 210 that ends the year for everyone else.

If under 210 are sold

The zombies lose, permanently, and the dogs live: the only living dogs that will ever exist are the ones nobody sold.

If 210 are sold

Two hundred and ten was enough. Everyone else loses: the apocalypse turns every living dog into a zombie the same night.

The confession

Nothing is waiting at the end

Staying on purpose is the piece.

I take no side in the ending.

The dog is the whole of what you get, forever, on purpose.

There is an easy way to build this: reward people for staying, and the year becomes a calculation. So I take no side. I get the same whether every dog lives or every dog is sold, and I gain nothing from a single dog dying. What you get for keeping your dog is the dog: alive, a day older every day, now and then handed something rare, and signed with your name if it dies of old age while it is still yours. What you get for selling is a corpse, and one more toward the 210. A thousand people, one dog each, one year, nobody rewarded for being good.

— Jonathan Washburn, 2026

The math is public. One finding held across every version of this year I tried: a pack that can panic will panic early. Nothing about the count fixes that, so the year gives it one clock instead. The apocalypse is checked at midnight and only at midnight, in a single batch: the cannon. The rule also carries a danger I would rather print at the gate than discover in public. Anyone who can talk two hundred and ten people into selling can end the year for everyone else, because every sale turns the dog it just moved. The only defense is a pack that will not list, and one dog per person means an attacker has to persuade two hundred and ten separate people instead of finding one wallet that holds a hundred. Adopting is the first vote.

The live design feed

Why this performance

Two reasons.

Building together.

I just launched a collection called HOLY, and I got to watch people flip the work: in and out in a day, never once looking at it. And that's fine. Flipping is how this market breathes; this game even needs its zombies. But I wanted to make holding mean something too. Here, selling a dog kills it, so staying is a real choice, made in public, with your name on the collar. Every dog that stays makes every other dog safer and scarcer. You can still flip. The piece just charges you the dog for it, and your sale counts toward the apocalypse hanging over everyone who stayed. A thousand strangers, one dog each, one year, one line to hold: that's the artwork.

Build together

The red button.

In April 2026, Tim Urban asked the internet a question:

Everyone in the world has to take a private vote by pressing a red or blue button. If more than 50% of people press the blue button, everyone survives. If less than 50% of people press the blue button, only people who pressed the red button survive. Which button would you press?
RED 42.1%
BLUE 57.9%

98,539 votes · 27.2 million views · final results

Blue won by a thread, and pressing was free. Forty-two percent chose red anyway. I couldn't stop thinking about what those numbers become when the vote costs something: your name on the button, a year to change your mind, and a count nobody can see. ALL DOGS is that question, played for keeps. Selling is the red button. Keeping is the blue one. The difference: red does not need half here. One dog in five, found any time inside a year, and it ends for everyone who held.

The method · whose art this is

Bred, not drawn.

"The idea becomes a machine that makes the art." Sol LeWitt wrote that in Paragraphs on Conceptual Art, 1967. This collection is that sentence, running.

I wrote a constitution for this collection: what a dog is here, what cuteness owes the viewer, how paint should behave, which painters set the bar and why none of them may be copied, the color law that separates the living from the dead. Every rule the machines obey, I authored.

Two machines execute it. The Painter rewrites the drawing code from scratch, round after round. The Critic reads the same constitution and judges the sheets blind against a gallery bar, never knowing which one is the champion. Only the better painter survives the round. Neither one can stop. Neither one can lower the bar. And I do not touch the brush.

"The process is mechanical and should not be tampered with. It should run its course." — LeWitt, Sentences on Conceptual Art, no. 29

If that sounds like the artist left the room, the argument is sixty years old. LeWitt's wall drawings were executed by other people's hands, working from his written instructions, and nobody has ever doubted whose art they are. "All of the planning and decisions are made beforehand and the execution is a perfunctory affair." The instructions are the art. My drafters happen not to be human.

What I authored: the dogs, the fates, the census, the tenderness, the temperature of the grounds, the wall the score has to clear, and every mid-fight correction, published as it happens. What I could not author: the exact painting that survives. That part surprises me the same way it surprises you, and it is the part I wanted.

"The process is carried out blindly. There are many side effects that the artist cannot imagine." — LeWitt, Sentences, no. 28

The attributes

Five fates.
One collar.

Every dog starts alive. What it becomes isn't decided at adoption and isn't decided by the artist. It's decided by a thousand owners, one midnight at a time. Three fates are reachable while the year is running. The last two only exist if the zombies lose.

While the year runs

Faithful

Still alive, still yours. A faithful dog is one that has never moved: not sold, not gifted, not shuffled to another wallet. It is also the only fate that keeps changing. Every week a few living dogs are handed one more rare trait. Every day, three in every ten thousand of them die of old age, which is about one dog every three days at this size. Keeping is not standing still.

alive · still aging

Zombie

Sold, while the year was still running. Its owner listed it, the sale turned it in the same transaction, and that is signed on the collar forever. Zombies trade freely from then on and never change again. Every dog that has been turned is the horde, and its count is the number hanging over the year: two hundred and ten of them ends the world for everyone else.

turned · sold

Angel

Died of old age: every living dog carries the same tiny daily chance, about a hundred deaths across the pack in a year, chosen by nobody. A plaque is etched across the top of the piece with the number of days it lived and the handle of the owner who cared for it the whole way. Only one kind of owner can be on that plaque, because selling ends the life. Angels never count toward the 210, and the apocalypse cannot touch them.

Vampire

Most become vampires: black eyes, fangs, one drop of blood, the same dog wearing the dark version of itself. Immortal

The rare side of that same roll, one trade in ten. Instead of the fangs the dog is given a superpower, the only trait in the collection that cannot be rolled at adoption or handed out by the weekly drop. An immortal is a dog that was held through the entire war and then let go on purpose, which is the only way one can exist.

Fate

The primary attribute. A zombie exists because someone sold. An angel exists because time took the dog. A vampire or an immortal exists because someone let go after the war was already won. And faithful is the one fate that doesn't last: the death clock never stops, not even after the year, so a dog kept long enough becomes an angel. Only a dog that is traded gets to stop.

Lived

Every collar carries a number: how many days the dog lived. A zombie is stamped the day it turned. An angel is stamped the day it passed, and that number goes up on the plaque too. Vampires and immortals stop on the day they were traded. The faithful just keep counting, long after the year ends. Day 3 (turned before its first walk) and day 3,650 (ten years old) are both rare; the middle belongs to everyone.

Traits

Once a week, a handful of living dogs are handed one more trait, and only rare ones: roughly a hundred dogs a year across the whole pack. You cannot buy into the drop, you cannot improve your odds, and the only way to end it yourself is to sell. It is the one thing in the collection that rewards you for doing nothing at all, and a dog that has been held a long time looks like it.

Wardrobe

The part decided at adoption. Each dog's seed rolls three independent slots (headwear, worn, held) at the odds printed below, published before the first dog exists. Every item carries its own accent color, and the held items are interactions: the dog reads the book, hugs the balloon, holds the drawing up for you. The crown is a 0.09% roll, which across a thousand dogs most likely means one or none. Twenty dogs are one of ones whose costumes ignore the first two slots, but even they roll the held one. A crowned zombie with a pocket knife is a story nobody can plan.

The plaque

Only angels get one, etched across the top of the piece: the days the dog lived, and the Twitter handle of the owner who cared for it its whole natural life. It is the only mark on any dog that names a person for what they did rather than what they own, and it cannot be forged or added later, because a dog that was ever sold is a zombie and a zombie never dies of old age.

Your handle on the collar. The fate. The days lived. The wardrobe it was born with and whatever the weekly drop added to it. The plaque, if it earned one. The drawing itself, one of one from its seed.

These are odds, not quotas, and a thousand dogs is a small enough pack that the rarest ones may simply never be dealt. Every dog rolls each slot on its own, so the crown is a 0.09% roll: across a full house that is most likely a single crown, and perfectly possibly none at all. Nobody finds out until the last dog is adopted. And the slots multiply. A crowned dog holding a pocket knife is 0.09% × 0.1%, about one in a million, which in a pack this size means it is almost certainly not coming. Even the one of ones roll the held slot, so The Carpenter holding a pocket knife is one specific dog times 0.1%, a piece that will probably never exist, and everyone will know the odds it beat if it does. Everything above is rolled the day you adopt. The weekly drop, the fate and the days lived are the opposite: nobody knows those until the year, and the years after it, write them. Half the rarity is dealt. The other half is earned by staying.

The medium

The paint is code.

There is no image file. Every dog is painted in your browser, right now, by a JavaScript painter working from a single number. And because it is code, not a picture, the same dog repaints at any size you ask: the avatar on your phone and a hundred-inch gallery print are the same strokes, laid down fresh, sharp at both.

One seed, three lives. The same dog, seed 910007, painted by the same code through each fate the year can hand it. The game never swaps an image. It repaints the soul.

Deterministic

The same seed paints the same dog, stroke for stroke, on any machine, in any decade. Your dog can be repainted from scratch a century from now and not one bristle moves.

Painterly by arithmetic

Thousands of layered strokes with seeded jitter in hue, pressure and dryness. Pigment behavior, computed. Zoom in: the edges break like a dry brush because the code says they should.

Infinite resolution

Code doesn't pixelate. The identical instructions paint a postage stamp or a two-meter gallery print, and the print is not a blowup, it's a fresh painting at that scale.

The chain finishes it

The seed decides the dog and its first wardrobe. The weeks add to it. The year decides its fate, and the day it dies is etched on the plaque with the name of whoever kept it. The blockchain doesn't store this art, it completes it.

No servers, no files

Nothing to host, nothing to pin, nothing to rot. A number and a painter, and the painting exists wherever somebody runs them.

The drop

Adopting works like a gallery,
not a gas war.

No bots, no gas auctions, no refreshing at midnight. One verified handle, one dog, one honest reading of the list.

01

Verify your handle.

Adoption is one dog per confirmed Twitter user, with no exceptions and no second wallets. That is the rule the whole year rests on: a thousand dogs means a thousand different people, not a hundred people holding ten each. Your handle goes on the collar and stays there forever.

02

Adoption day.

One day, announced ahead of time. The adoption fee is $50. A hold goes on your card and nothing is charged yet. Nobody races anybody: everyone who shows up that day stands in the same line, because there is nothing to race for when everybody is asking for exactly one dog.

03

If more than a thousand ask.

The list is read, not raced. Places get filled by the history of the wallet you come with: art held for years counts for you, quick flips count against you. The dogs go home with people who keep things, because a year of this game is entirely about who keeps things.

The pack is however many dogs find homes that day. If that's short of 1,000, the year plays exactly the same and the apocalypse still comes at 21% of the dogs that were actually adopted. Dogs nobody adopts are never born at all.

The year

How the year works

01

Adopt a dog. The adoption fee is $50. One per confirmed Twitter account.

One handle, one dog, no second wallets. The fee is taken once, on the day you adopt, and every count in the game scales to the pack that actually got adopted. A thousand dogs means a thousand different people, which is the only reason the rest of this is a game rather than an auction.

02

A dog you keep does not sit still.

Two clocks run on every living dog. Once a week a handful of them are handed one more trait, and only rare ones, roughly a hundred dogs a year across the pack. Every day, three in every ten thousand living dogs die of old age and become angels, which is about a hundred a year as well. Nobody chooses either one and nothing you do changes your odds. Holding is not a waiting room: it is the only way your dog gets rarer, and it is also the only way it dies with your name on it.

03

An angel is signed with the name of whoever kept it.

When a dog dies of old age, a plaque is etched across the top of the piece carrying the number of days it lived and the Twitter handle of the owner who cared for it its whole natural life. Only one kind of owner can ever appear there, because selling ends the life before it can end naturally. The plaque is the proof you never sold, and it is the only thing in the collection that cannot be added later.

04

Selling your dog kills it.

Not as a penalty. As the mechanism. For the whole of the first year, the moment a living dog leaves your wallet the contract turns it into a zombie in the same transaction, and the buyer receives the corpse. There is no button, no confirmation and no way to sell a dog and keep it alive. The turn is signed on the collar forever, your handle still on it, and you have joined the horde's side of the count: one of the two hundred and ten that ends the year for everyone still holding. Selling out and betraying the pack are the same act here. That's the piece.

05

Nobody can buy a living dog.

Not from anyone, not on any day of the year. Every dog that changes hands is already dead or dies on delivery, so the only living dogs in existence are the ones sitting in the wallets that adopted them. That makes holding the one thing a listing cannot give you, and it makes the listing honest: a listed dog is a public announcement that its owner is about to kill it. Zombies are the whole supply, so the better the pack holds, the scarcer the dead get, and the louder every listing whispers at everyone still holding.

06

At 210 zombies, the apocalypse.

If 21% of the pack has been sold at any point inside the year, the horde takes every living dog on the next midnight and they come back as zombies: still yours, tradable, never alive again. Two hundred and ten sales in three hundred and sixty five days works out to one sale every 42 hours, sustained all year, which is an ordinary amount of churn and is exactly why the line is set there. Angels are already gone and cannot be taken.

07

Survive the year and the zombies lose forever.

If the last midnight passes with the count under 210, no dog is ever turned into a zombie by a sale again, and every dog still in its adopting wallet is a living animal that nobody could have bought on any day of that year. That is all that happens, and it is the good ending.

08

After that, letting go costs the life instead of ending it.

Once the zombies have lost, everything trades freely, but a living dog still cannot be traded and stay living. It stops aging, stops collecting traits, and becomes one of two things: a vampire, which is the same dog with black eyes, fangs and a drop of blood, or one time in ten an immortal, which is handed a superpower no other dog can roll. Either way the clock stops, so it will never be an angel and will never carry a plaque. The choice never actually goes away. It just changes what it costs.

09

The world can only end at midnight.

Every sale is public the second it happens, so anyone can watch the count climb all day. What no one can do is end the year early. The contract checks the count once every midnight and fires in a single batch, like the cannon in the Hunger Games. Some nights it's silence. Some nights it's forty dogs. You can see the axe. You never know which midnight it falls.
