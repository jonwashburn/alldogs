ALL DOGS™

  

1,000 dogs · one zombie apocalypse · adoption fee $50 · one per person

ALL DOGS is a ninety-day performance played by a thousand strangers holding one dog each. The artwork is whatever is left when the ninety days end. Every dog is its own drawing living on Ethereum: the artist draws the dogs, the owners write the story.

The game

Zombie apocalypse

A thousand people each adopt one dog and keep it for ninety days. A dog you keep grows older every day and rarer every week. Selling it kills it: for those whole ninety days, the moment a living dog changes hands the contract turns it into a zombie. If 150 dogs are sold inside them, the apocalypse comes and every dog still living turns with them.

1

Start by adopting your dog. $50 at the gate, one dog per confirmed handle. Your handle goes on the collar.

2

Then decide: keep it, or kill it. Selling is the only way to kill it, and it always does. The buyer receives a corpse, so nobody can ever buy a living dog at any price, and your sale is one of the 150 that ends it for everyone else.

If under 150 are sold

The zombies lose, permanently, and the dogs live: the only living dogs that will ever exist are the ones nobody sold.

If 150 are sold

A hundred and fifty was enough. Everyone else loses: the apocalypse turns every living dog into a zombie the same night.

This map runs the ninety days, then starts them again, for as long as you watch it. Nothing in it is scripted: every night, every living dog rolls the same small chance of dying that the rules give it, and somebody sells at the rate on the slider. Only a dog's first sale turns it red, because a dog can only be given up once. The slider sits on the line itself, one sale every 14 hours, close enough that some runs end in a graveyard and some end in a cannon. Move it and see how little it takes.

Why this performance

Two reasons.

Building together.

I just launched a collection called HOLY, and I got to watch people flip the work: in and out in a day, never once looking at it. And that's fine. Flipping is how this market breathes; this game even needs its zombies. But I wanted to make holding mean something too. Here, selling a dog kills it, so staying is a real choice, made in public, with your name on the collar. Every dog that stays makes every other dog safer and scarcer. You can still flip. The piece just charges you the dog for it, and your sale counts toward the apocalypse hanging over everyone who stayed. A thousand strangers, one dog each, ninety days, one line to hold: that's the artwork.

Build together

The red button.

In April 2026, Tim Urban asked the internet a question:

Everyone in the world has to take a private vote by pressing a red or blue button. If more than 50% of people press the blue button, everyone survives. If less than 50% of people press the blue button, only people who pressed the red button survive. Which button would you press?
RED 42.1%
BLUE 57.9%

98,539 votes · 27.2 million views · final results

Blue won by a thread, and pressing was free. Forty-two percent chose red anyway. I couldn't stop thinking about what those numbers become when the vote costs something: your name on the button, a ninety days to change your mind, and a running count everyone can see. ALL DOGS is that question, played for keeps. Selling is the red button. Keeping is the blue one. The difference: red does not need half here. One dog in seven, found any time inside ninety days, and it ends for everyone who held.

The method · whose art this is

Bred, not drawn.

"The idea becomes a machine that makes the art." Sol LeWitt wrote that in Paragraphs on Conceptual Art, 1967. This collection is that sentence, running.

I wrote a constitution for this collection: what a dog is here, what cuteness owes the viewer, how paint should behave, which painters set the bar and why none of them may be copied, the color law that separates the living from the dead. Every rule the machines obey, I authored.

Two machines execute it. The Painter rewrites the drawing code from scratch, round after round. The Critic reads the same constitution and judges the sheets blind against a gallery bar, never knowing which one is the champion. Only the better painter survives the round. Neither one can stop. Neither one can lower the bar. And I do not touch the brush.

"The process is mechanical and should not be tampered with. It should run its course." — LeWitt, Sentences on Conceptual Art, no. 29

What I authored: the dogs, the fates, the census, the tenderness, the temperature of the grounds, the wall the score has to clear, and every mid-fight correction, published as it happens. What I could not author: the exact painting that survives. That part surprises me the same way it surprises you, and it is the part I wanted.

"The process is carried out blindly. There are many side effects that the artist cannot imagine." — LeWitt, Sentences, no. 28

The attributes

Five fates.
One collar.

Every dog starts alive. What it becomes isn't decided at adoption and isn't decided by the artist. It's decided by a thousand owners, one midnight at a time. Three fates are reachable while the ninety days run. The last two only exist if the zombies lose.

While the ninety days run

Faithful

Still alive, still yours. A faithful dog is one that has never moved: not sold, not gifted, not shuffled to another wallet. It is also the only fate that keeps changing. Every week a few living dogs are handed one more rare trait. Every day, two in every ten thousand of them die of old age, which is about one dog every five days at this size. Keeping is not standing still.

alive · still aging

Zombie

Sold, while the ninety days were still running. Its owner listed it, the sale turned it in the same transaction, and that is signed on the collar forever. Zombies trade freely from then on and never change again, and none of that trading counts for anything: a dog turns on its first sale and can never turn twice. The horde is how many separate dogs have been given up, and that count is the number hanging over everything: a hundred and fifty of them ends the world for everyone else.

turned · sold

Angel

Died of old age: every living dog carries the same tiny daily chance, about seventy deaths across the pack in a year, chosen by nobody. A plaque is etched across the top of the piece with the number of days it lived and the handle of the owner who cared for it the whole way. Only one kind of owner can be on that plaque, because selling ends the life. Angels never count toward the 150, and the apocalypse cannot touch them.

Lived

Every collar carries a number: how many days the dog lived. A zombie is stamped the day it turned. An angel is stamped the day it passed, and that number goes up on the plaque too. Vampires and immortals stop on the day they were traded. The faithful just keep counting, long after the ninety days end. Day 3 (turned before its first walk) and day 3,650 (ten years old) are both rare; the middle belongs to everyone.

Wardrobe

The part decided at adoption. Each dog's seed rolls three independent slots (headwear, worn, held) at the odds printed below, published before the first dog exists. Every item carries its own accent color, and the held items are interactions: the dog reads the book, hugs the balloon, holds the drawing up for you. The crown is a 0.09% roll, which across a thousand dogs most likely means one or none. Twenty dogs are one of ones whose costumes ignore the first two slots, but even they roll the held one. A crowned zombie with a pocket knife is a story nobody can plan.

Your handle on the collar. The fate. The days lived. The wardrobe it was born with and whatever the weekly drop added to it. The plaque, if it earned one. The drawing itself, one of one from its seed.

These are odds, not quotas, and a thousand dogs is a small enough pack that the rarest ones may simply never be dealt. Every dog rolls each slot on its own, so the crown is a 0.09% roll: across a full house that is most likely a single crown, and perfectly possibly none at all. Nobody finds out until the last dog is adopted. And the slots multiply. A crowned dog holding a pocket knife is 0.09% × 0.1%, about one in a million, which in a pack this size means it is almost certainly not coming. Even the one of ones roll the held slot, so The Carpenter holding a pocket knife is one specific dog times 0.1%, a piece that will probably never exist, and everyone will know the odds it beat if it does. Everything above is rolled the day you adopt. The weekly drop, the fate and the days lived are the opposite: nobody knows those until the ninety days, and the years after them, write them. Half the rarity is dealt. The other half is earned by staying.

The medium

The paint is code.

There is no image file. Every dog is painted in your browser, right now, by a JavaScript painter working from a single number. And because it is code, not a picture, the same dog repaints at any size you ask: the avatar on your phone and a hundred-inch gallery print are the same strokes, laid down fresh, sharp at both.

One seed, three lives. The same dog, seed 910007, painted by the same code through each fate the ninety days can hand it. The game never swaps an image. It repaints the soul.

Deterministic

The seed reconstructs the same painting instructions, stroke for stroke, on any machine in any decade. What a given screen makes of them is a question for that screen, which is the same bargain every painting has always had with the light in the room.

Painterly by arithmetic

Thousands of layered strokes with seeded jitter in hue, pressure and dryness. Pigment behavior, computed. Zoom in: the edges break like a dry brush because the code says they should.

The chain finishes it

The seed decides the dog and its first wardrobe. The weeks add to it. The ninety days decide its fate, and the day it dies is etched on the plaque with the name of whoever kept it. The blockchain doesn't store this art, it completes it.

The composite

The face of the pack.

In 1877 Francis Galton stacked portrait photographs on one plate, giving each a fraction of the exposure, to find the face behind a group of faces. He meant that literally and he meant it badly: he coined the word eugenics, and the plates were an instrument for finding inherited criminality in the shape of a jaw. He never found it, because it is not there. What he had built instead was the first way to photograph a population rather than a person, and the technique outlived the whole ugly purpose of it.

It is worth taking off him on purpose. His composites were meant to show what people were born as. This one shows what a thousand people decided to do. The face of the living exists because those particular owners did not sell, and it would be a different face if they had.

Which is the only way to see this artwork at all. It is not any one dog. It is what a thousand strangers did over ninety days, every dog a brushstroke in it, and there has never been a way to look at that directly. This is the picture of the whole painting.

Pick a fate and watch it build. The dogs go in one at a time to start with, slowly enough to see each one arrive and lose itself, then faster as it stops mattering which one is which. Every dog on this page belongs to somebody. This one cannot: an average has no owner, which makes it the only thing here that belongs to all of them at once.

The drop

Nobody gets in
who wasn't let in.

There is no public sale. The first dogs go to people who already collect my work, and from there the pack lets itself in, three at a time.

01

It starts with my collectors.

No form, no application, no raffle. If you already hold something of mine, a dog is waiting for you at $50, one per confirmed Twitter handle like everybody else. That first wave is the only part of the pack I choose.

02

Adopt, and you get three invites.

Three Twitter handles, named by you, each getting exactly the deal you got: one dog, fifty dollars, one handle on the collar. The moment they adopt they get three invites of their own. After the first wave, every place in this collection is handed over by somebody who already took one.

03

There is no other door.

No public day, no bots, no gas auction, nothing to refresh at midnight, and no way to buy your way to the front. Three invites each fills a thousand in two or three passes, and when the last dog goes the door shuts.

Which means every dog in the pack was let in by somebody already in it, so the thousand are a chain and not a crowd. That matters more here than it would anywhere else. The ninety days are a test of whether a thousand people can hold together, so how the thousand were assembled is part of the experiment, and a gas war would have assembled strangers. In a chain, every choice has somebody standing one link away who made it possible.

If the pack never fills, the ninety days play exactly the same and the apocalypse still comes at 15% of the dogs that were actually adopted. Dogs nobody adopts are never born at all.

The ninety days

How the ninety days work

01

A dog you keep does not sit still.

Two clocks run on every living dog. Once a week a handful of them are handed one more trait, and only rare ones, roughly a hundred dogs a year across the pack. Every day, two in every ten thousand living dogs die of old age and become angels, which is about seventy a year. Nobody chooses either one and nothing you do changes your odds. Holding is not a waiting room: it is the only way your dog gets rarer, and it is also the only way it dies with your name on it.

02

Selling your dog kills it.

Not as a penalty. As the mechanism. For the whole of the first ninety days, the moment a living dog leaves your wallet the contract turns it into a zombie in the same transaction, and the buyer receives the corpse. There is no button, no confirmation and no way to sell a dog and keep it alive. The turn is signed on the collar forever, your handle still on it, and you have joined the horde's side of the count: one of the hundred and fifty that ends it for everyone still holding. It is what makes a listing honest: a listed dog is a public announcement that its owner is about to kill it.

03

At 150 first sales, the apocalypse.

The count is first sales only. A dog can turn once and only once, on the day it first leaves the wallet that adopted it, and after that it is a zombie that trades as often as anyone likes without ever moving the number again. So the count is not volume and it cannot be manufactured: it is how many separate dogs have been given up, out of the thousand. If 15% of the pack, a hundred and fifty of them, has been given up at any point inside the ninety days, the horde takes every living dog on the next midnight and they come back as zombies: still yours, tradable, never alive again. A hundred and fifty first sales in ninety days works out to one sale every 14 hours, sustained the whole way, which is an ordinary amount of churn for a new collection and is exactly why the line is set there. Angels are already gone and cannot be taken.

04

Survive the ninety days and the zombies lose forever.

If the last midnight passes with the count under 150, no dog is ever turned into a zombie by a sale again, and every dog still in its adopting wallet is a living animal that nobody could have bought on any day of them. A living dog still cannot be traded and stay living, so from here letting go makes a vampire, or one time in ten an immortal. That is all that happens, and it is the good ending.
