ALL DOGS™

  

10,000 dogs · one zombie apocalypse · adoption fee $25

ALL DOGS is a year-long performance played with real money by up to 10,000 strangers, and the artwork is whatever is left when the year ends. Every dog is a one-of-one drawing that lives on Ethereum. The artist draws the dogs. The owners write the story.

How the year works Meet the four fates Watch the machine draw

The game

Zombie apocalypse

Start by adopting your dog.

Then decide: love it, or flip it.

If 2/3 of dog owners love their dogs, everyone wins.

If more than 1/3 put their dogs down for the money, everyone else loses.

Why this performance

Two reasons.

Fuck flippers.

I just launched a collection called HOLY, and I got to watch people flip the work: in and out in a day, never once looking at it. So I built something a flipper cannot touch. Here, a living dog can never be sold. There is no floor to sweep and no exit to time. The only way to cash out is to kill your own dog, with your name on the collar, in public, forever. You can still flip. But the piece will make you sign the kill, and it counts toward the apocalypse hanging over every dog that stayed.

Fuck flippers

The red button.

In April 2026, Tim Urban asked the internet a question:

Everyone in the world has to take a private vote by pressing a red or blue button. If more than 50% of people press the blue button, everyone survives. If less than 50% of people press the blue button, only people who pressed the red button survive. Which button would you press?
RED 42.1%
BLUE 57.9%

98,539 votes · 27.2 million views · final results

Blue won by a thread, and pressing was free. Forty-two percent chose red anyway. I couldn't stop thinking about what those numbers become when the vote costs something: real money, your name on the button, a year to change your mind, and a count nobody can see. ALL DOGS is that question, played for keeps. Turn is the red button. Hold is the blue one. The one mercy I added: here, red needs a third.

The attributes

Four fates.
One collar.

Every dog starts alive. What it becomes isn't decided at mint and isn't decided by the artist. It's decided by 10,000 owners, one midnight at a time.

Faithful

Still alive, still yours. A faithful dog is one whose owner said no to the horde every single night. If the dogs win the year, the faithful get refunded and trade free.

alive · holding the line

Zombie

Turned by its own owner. The owner took the money, the horde took the dog, and the turn is signed on the collar forever. Zombies trade from the night they turn.

turned · paid

Angel

Died of natural causes, a few every week, chosen by chance and nobody else. Wings included, the nights it lived stamped on the collar. Angels never count toward the third. They trade right away, and if the dogs win the year they get refunded too.

at peace · tradable

Ghost

A dog the apocalypse took. If the horde ever reaches a third, every living dog is taken that same night and comes back under a sheet. Ghosts trade, but no refund ever comes.

taken · never paid
Fate

The primary attribute. A zombie exists because someone chose the money. A ghost exists because the world fell. An angel exists because nature took the dog. And faithful is the one fate that doesn't last: the death clock never stops, not even after the year, so someday every faithful dog becomes an angel. The collection ends in wings.

Lived

Every collar carries a number: how many nights the dog lived. A zombie is stamped the night it turned. An angel is stamped the night it passed. Ghosts all share one terrible date. The faithful just keep counting, long after the year ends. Night 3 (turned before its first walk) and night 3,650 (ten years old) are both rare; the middle belongs to everyone.

Wardrobe

The one thing decided at mint. Each dog's seed rolls three independent slots (headwear, worn, held) at the odds printed below, published before the first dog exists. Every item carries its own accent color, and the held items are interactions: the dog reads the book, hugs the balloon, holds the drawing up for you. The crown is a 0.09% roll. Twelve dogs are one of ones whose costumes ignore the first two slots, but even they roll the held one. A crowned zombie with a pocket knife is a story nobody can plan.

On the piece

Your handle on the collar, inscribed if you reserved. The fate. The nights lived. The wardrobe. The drawing itself, one of one from its seed.

These are odds, not quotas. Every dog rolls each slot on its own, so the crown is a 0.09% roll: expect about nine at a full house, but the dice don't owe anybody nine. And the slots multiply. A crowned dog holding a pocket knife is 0.09% × 0.1%: roughly one dog in a million, which almost certainly means zero and might mean one. Even the one of ones roll the held slot. The Carpenter holding a pocket knife is one specific dog times 0.1%, a piece that will probably never exist, and everyone will know the odds it beat if it does. The fate and lived tables are the opposite of all this: nobody knows those until the year, and the years after it, write them. Half the rarity is rolled. The other half is earned.

The medium

The paint is code.

There is no image file. Every dog is painted in your browser, right now, by a JavaScript painter working from a single number. The whole painter is about a hundred kilobytes: smaller than one photograph of the painting it makes.

One seed, four lives. The same dog, seed 910007, painted by the same code through each fate the year could hand it. The game never swaps an image. It repaints the soul.

Deterministic

The same seed paints the same dog, stroke for stroke, on any machine, in any decade. Your dog can be repainted from scratch a century from now and not one bristle moves.

Painterly by arithmetic

Thousands of layered strokes with seeded jitter in hue, pressure and dryness. Pigment behavior, computed. Zoom in: the edges break like a dry brush because the code says they should.

Bred, not drawn

The painter itself was evolved in public: one AI repaints the collection, a second judges it blind against a gallery bar, and only the better painter survives the round.

Watch it live
Infinite resolution

Code doesn't pixelate. The identical instructions paint a postage stamp or a two-meter gallery print, and the print is not a blowup, it's a fresh painting at that scale.

The chain finishes it

The seed decides the dog and its wardrobe. The year decides its fate. The night it dies stamps its collar. The blockchain doesn't store this art, it completes it.

No servers, no files

Nothing to host, nothing to pin, nothing to rot. A number and a painter, and the painting exists wherever somebody runs them.

The drop

Adopting works like a gallery,
not a gas war.

No bots, no gas auctions, no refreshing at midnight. One reservation day, one honest allocation, then a public day for whatever's left.

01

Reservation day.

One day, announced ahead of time. Verify your Twitter handle and reserve up to 100 dogs, adoption fee $25 each. A hold goes on your card and nothing is charged yet. Nobody races anybody: everyone who shows up that day stands in the same line.

02

The allocation.

Orders are read, not raced. Reservations get filled by wallet history: art held for years counts for you, quick flips count against you. The dogs go home with people who keep them. If your order fills, your handle is inscribed on every collar, forever.

03

Public adoption day.

Whatever's left opens to everyone, first come, first served, no handle needed. Launch-day dogs play the same year and live the same lives; their collars just don't carry a name.

The pack is however many dogs find homes that week. If that's short of 10,000, the year plays exactly the same and the apocalypse still comes at one third of the pack. Dogs nobody adopts are never born at all.

The year

How the year works

01

Adopt dogs. The adoption fee is $25. Up to 100 per verified Twitter account.

Adoption runs in three steps, printed above: reservation day, the allocation, then a public day for whatever's left. Every dollar the year collects sits in the contract where anyone can look at it, and every count in the game scales to the pack that actually got adopted.

02

A living dog can never be sold.

Not to a friend, not to a museum, not for any price. If you want to sell your dog, there is exactly one way: put it down. Turn it into a zombie, take the $20, and your zombie trades that same night, free to flip. But understand what you bought with that: the turn is signed on the collar forever, and you've joined the zombies' side of the count, one dog closer to the apocalypse for every dog still holding. Selling out and betraying the pack are the same button here. That's the piece.

03

Turning your dog pays $20,* tonight.

Any night from the day you adopt, you can hand your dog to the horde and take back the $20 of ethereum that was locked for your dog on adoption day. It pays on the spot. Your new zombie trades immediately, your handle still on its collar. The turn is signed.

04

Hold the line for a year and everybody gets paid.

The same $20 the killers take is waiting for every living dog. If the last midnight passes and the horde never reached a third, every living dog collects it, trades free, and is marked faithful forever. The artist loses his bet. That's the good ending.

05

At one third, the apocalypse.

If a third of the dogs are ever turned, the horde takes every living dog that same night. They come back as ghosts: still yours, tradable, never refunded. The $20 that was waiting for each of them goes to the artist instead. Natural deaths never count toward the third.

06

The horde count updates once a night.

You never see turns in real time. They're sealed as they happen, and once every midnight the new count is published in a single batch, like the cannon in the Hunger Games. Some nights it's silence. Some nights it's forty dogs. Between cannons, nobody on earth knows how close the third is.

If the apocalypse comes, the two thirds that wasn't already paid out to the zombies comes to me instead of back to you: at most, $20 × 10,000 × ⅔ ≈ $133,000. The $5 of every adoption fee pays for the drawings, the fees, and the year of running it, and I keep that no matter how the year ends. My position is printed at the gate on purpose: every turned dog is a donation to the person telling you not to. The only way to beat me is together.

The math is public. Every rule on this page gets run through an agent-based economy simulator for thousands of runs before any contract is written. Two findings so far: hiding the count doesn't stop a panic, it makes the panic preemptive, which is why the cannon exists. And ordinary greed never reaches the third on its own; only an organized army does. Buying in is the first vote.
