The pack · no first dog, no lastDrag the row · open any dog

Seven days to adopt · one zombie apocalypse · adoption fee $50 · one per person

ALL DOGS is the second half of a work about what a record does to a life. The first half, HOLY, is an adolescence whose album was lost, described from memory to a machine; the only original is the camera. This half is the album that cannot be lost: a ninety-day performance played by friends of friends, one dog each, every move signed on the collar. There is no edition size. The door is open for seven days and the pack is however many people walk through it, which means nobody knows how big this is, including me. The artwork is whatever is left when the ninety days end. Every dog is its own drawing living on Ethereum: the artist draws the dogs, the owners write the story.

The game

Zombie apocalypse

Dog people, each let in by somebody already here, adopt one dog each and keep it for ninety days. A dog you keep grows older every day and rarer every week. Selling it kills it: for the whole ninety days, the moment a living dog changes hands the contract turns it into a zombie. If 15% of the pack has been sold before the ninety days are up, the apocalypse comes and every living dog turns that same night.

Why this performance

One collection had no record.
This one forgets nothing.

Nothing left to attach.

My last collection is a lost adolescence. An evangelical coming of age between 1975 and 1995 that was never simple enough to hate: church buses, revival carpet, motel pools, baptisms, hard flash. Almost no photographs survived it. Most of the album is gone.

So I told a machine to restore the attached photos, and there was nothing left to attach. What came back is the collection. There is no original anywhere in it. The only object in the world that light from those years actually touched is the camera.

Making it left me with a question I could not put down. If a life ends up being whatever the record kept, what happens to one where the record keeps everything?

Everything is written down.

So this one forgets nothing. Your handle is on the collar from the first day. Sell, and the sale is the kill, and the turn is signed there forever. Hold, and if the dog dies of old age while it is still yours, a plaque carries the days it lived and your name, and the only way to get one is to have never let go. There is no move here that goes unwritten.

Which turns owning a thing into doing a thing. Holding stops being a position and becomes something you are doing to an animal, in public, beside everyone else who was vouched for the same way you were, with the count of who gave up visible all day. Whether a crowd of strangers will hold together is an old question, and a dull one, because the answer is usually no. Whether a tribe will is the question worth paying for.

One collection is what I could not keep. This one is a room full of people finding out what they can.

HOLY and ALL DOGS are one work in two halves: the album that kept nothing, and the album that cannot lose anything. The camera and the collar are the same object a generation apart, the one thing that actually touched the life. And the angels leave the screen. Every plaque a dog earns by dying held is engraved in brass, with the days and the handle, and hung in a room. The only way onto that wall is never to have let go.

The method · whose art this is

Bred, not drawn.

"The idea becomes a machine that makes the art." Sol LeWitt wrote that in Paragraphs on Conceptual Art, 1967. This collection is that sentence, running.

I wrote a constitution for this collection: what a dog is here, what cuteness owes the viewer, how paint should behave, which painters set the bar and why none of them may be copied, the color law that separates the living from the dead. Every rule the machines obey, I authored.

Six machines execute it. Five painters rewrite the drawing code from scratch, all at once, each against the same champion. One critic reads the same constitution and judges the sheets blind against a gallery bar, never knowing which one is the champion. A sheet replaces the champion only by beating it. None of them can stop. None of them can lower the bar. And I do not touch the brush.

"The process is mechanical and should not be tampered with. It should run its course." LeWitt, Sentences on Conceptual Art, no. 29

What I authored: the dogs, the fates, the odds, the tenderness, the temperature of the grounds, the score a painter has to clear, and every mid-fight correction, published as it happens. What I could not author: the exact painting that survives. That part surprises me the same way it surprises you, and it is the part I wanted.

"The process is carried out blindly. There are many side effects that the artist cannot imagine." LeWitt, Sentences, no. 28

The fight does not stop when the door shuts. What a dog is freezes at the reveal: its type, its coat, its wardrobe, its one of one, so the odds printed below are a contract. How it is painted keeps being fought over, in public, for the same ninety days the holding is fought over, and both freeze together at the last midnight. Two wars, one end. The painter that is champion that night is the painter forever.

Every round of the fight redraws the whole collection, so the same seed comes out a different animal each time. This is that one seed, from the first round to the newest, with nothing in between removed except the rounds that drew nothing at all.

Watch the number while you watch the dog. The critic scores blind, out of eighty (out of seventy before cuteness was a line on the card), and it has never once been told what the collection is supposed to look like beyond the constitution. The score goes up as the pictures get rougher. The neat little portrait near the start is not an earlier attempt at what we have now. It is the thing we had, and left.

Competence was the easy part and it arrived early. Anyone can point this machine at a smooth commercial dog and it will paint one all day. That is not what is being selected for here, and the strip shows it: the pictures are getting stranger, looser and more sure of themselves, and the blind judge keeps raising the score.

The attributes

Five fates.
One collar.

Every dog starts alive. The owners decide what it becomes, one midnight at a time; the adoption roll has no say in it and neither does the artist. Three fates are reachable while the ninety days run. The last two only exist if the zombies lose.

While the ninety days run

Faithful

Still alive, still yours. A faithful dog is one that has never moved: not sold, not gifted, not shuffled to another wallet. It is also the only fate that keeps changing. Every week a few living dogs are handed one more rare trait. Every day, two in every ten thousand of them die of old age. Keeping is not standing still.

alive · still aging

Zombie

Sold, while the ninety days were still running. Its owner listed it, the sale turned it in the same transaction, and that is signed on the collar forever. Zombies trade freely from then on and never change again, and none of that trading counts for anything: a dog turns on its first sale and can never turn twice. The horde is how many separate dogs have been given up, and that count is the number hanging over everything: fifteen in every hundred adopted ends the world for everyone else.

turned · sold

Angel

Died of old age. Every living dog carries the same tiny daily chance, about seven in a hundred over a year, and nobody chooses which ones it lands on. A plaque is etched across the top of the piece with the number of days it lived and the handle of the owner who kept it the whole way. Only a keeper can be on that plaque, because selling ends the life, and every plaque is also cut in brass and hung in a room. Angels never count toward the 15%, and the apocalypse cannot touch them.

at peace · signed

Only if the zombies lose

Vampire

If the ninety days end under 15%, no dog is ever turned into a zombie by a sale again and everything trades freely. But a living dog still cannot be traded and stay living. Most become vampires: black eyes, fangs, one drop of blood, the same dog wearing the dark version of itself. It stops aging and stops collecting traits, so it can never become an angel and can never grow again.

stopped · after the war

Immortal

The rare side of that same trade, one in ten. Instead of the fangs the dog is given a superpower, the only trait in the collection that cannot be rolled at adoption or handed out by the weekly drop. An immortal is a dog that was held through the entire war and then let go on purpose. There is no other way to make one.

stopped · superpowered

The same dog. The buttons change what happened to it. Another of the one hundred and sixteen takes its place in a few seconds.

Lived

Every collar carries a number: how many days the dog lived. A zombie is stamped the day it turned. An angel is stamped the day it passed, and that number goes up on the plaque too. Vampires and immortals stop on the day they were traded. The faithful just keep counting, long after the ninety days end. Day 3 (turned before its first walk) and day 3,650 (ten years old) are both rare; the middle belongs to everyone.

Wardrobe

The part decided at adoption. Each dog's seed rolls three independent slots (headwear, worn, held) at the odds printed below, published before the first dog exists. Every item carries its own accent color, and a held item is something the dog does: it sips the martini, hugs the balloon, holds the Polaroid up for you. The crown is a 0.09% roll, so whether this pack gets one at all depends on how many people came through the door. One hundred and sixteen dogs are one of ones whose costumes ignore the first two slots, but even they roll the held one. A crowned zombie with a martini is a story nobody can plan.

One hundred and sixteen dogs in the pack are somebody. Ninety-one of them are people: pop saints, presidents, painters with their one object, two who built companies, six athletes in blank jerseys. Twenty-five are dogs: the famous ones, who waited at a station, listened at a horn, went up in a capsule, lay on a grave, and are what this collection is about. Each one carries a year and nothing else. The year is when the costume happened, and the whole game is working out whose costume it is. The first one is 33; the oldest is a dog in a courtyard, and its year has a minus sign; three years name two dogs each, and then the costume is all you get. Nobody will get all of them and nobody is meant to. There are exactly one hundred and sixteen no matter how many dogs get adopted, so a small pack makes them common and a big one makes them nearly impossible, and nobody knows which until the door shuts. They are drawn by lot at the close, over the whole pack at once, so adopting early buys nothing. These are also the only things in the collection that cannot be earned: no weekly drop will ever hand one out, while everything else on this page can still arrive years from now.

The rest are odds, not quotas. Every dog rolls each slot on its own, so the crown is a 0.09% roll and the number of crowns depends entirely on how many people walk through the door: at four hundred dogs there is most likely no crown in the world, and at four thousand there are three or four. Nobody finds out until the last dog is adopted, and that is the part you cannot buy your way around. The slots also multiply. A crowned dog holding a martini is 0.09% × 0.1%, about one in a million, so at any pack size we are likely to see it is almost certainly not coming. Even the one of ones roll the held slot, so the one from 33 holding a martini is one specific dog times 0.1%, a piece that will probably never exist, and everyone will know the odds it beat if it does. Everything above is settled at the close. The weekly drop, the fate and the days lived are the opposite: nobody knows those until the ninety days, and the years after them, have written them. Half the rarity is dealt. The other half is earned by staying.

Deterministic

The seed reconstructs the same painting instructions, stroke for stroke, on any machine in any decade. What a given screen makes of them is a question for that screen, which is the same bargain every painting has always had with the light in the room.

Painterly by arithmetic

Thousands of layered strokes with seeded jitter in hue, pressure and dryness. Pigment behavior, computed. Zoom in: the edges break like a dry brush because the code says they should.

The composite

The face of the pack.

In 1877 Francis Galton stacked portrait photographs on one plate, giving each a fraction of the exposure, to find the face behind a group of faces. He meant that literally and he meant it badly: he coined the word eugenics, and the plates were an instrument for finding inherited criminality in the shape of a jaw. He never found it, because it is not there. What he had built instead was the first way to photograph a population rather than a person, and the technique outlived the whole ugly purpose of it.

Turning his instrument against his purpose is deliberate. His composites were meant to show what people were born as. This one shows what a group of people decided to do. The face of the living exists because those particular owners did not sell, and it would be a different face if they had.

It is also the only way to see this artwork whole. No single dog is the work. The work is what a pack of dog people did over ninety days, every dog a brushstroke in it, and there has never been a way to look at that directly. This is the picture of the whole painting.

Ninety nights. Once the door shuts this stops being a demo. Every midnight, after the cannon, the face of the living is built from the real pack, the dogs still in the wallets that adopted them that night, and kept. On the ninetieth night there are ninety faces of one tribe turning, dying and holding, and that sequence is the one token I mint for myself: the only piece in the collection that belongs to nobody in the pack, and the record of the record.

The drop

Nobody gets in
who wasn't let in.

There is no public sale and there is no edition size. The first dogs go to people who already collect my work. From there the pack lets itself in three at a time, and after seven days the door shuts on whoever made it through.

01

It starts with my collectors.

No form, no application, no raffle. If you already hold something of mine, a dog is waiting for you at $50, one per confirmed Twitter handle like everybody else. That first wave is the only part of the pack I choose.

02

Adopt, and you get three invites.

Three Twitter handles, named by you, each getting exactly the deal you got: one dog, fifty dollars, one handle on the collar. The moment they adopt they get three invites of their own. After the first wave, every place in this collection is handed over by somebody who already took one.

03

Seven days, and no other door.

No public day, no bots, no gas auction, nothing to refresh at midnight, and no way to buy your way to the front. There is no number of dogs to sell out of, so nothing runs out and nothing is won by being fast. The only scarce thing is the week. When it ends the door shuts, the pack is however many walked through it, and that number is fixed forever.

Which means every dog in the pack was let in by somebody already in it, so the pack is a chain and not a crowd. That matters more here than it would anywhere else. The ninety days are a test of whether a group of people can hold together, so how the group was assembled is part of the experiment. A gas war buys a crowd of strangers. This buys a tribe: dog people, friends of friends, every one of them standing one link from somebody who vouched for them.

Nobody knows how big this gets, and that includes me. An edition size is a promise an artist makes about how much attention the work deserves, and I would rather find out. Every rule downstream is a share rather than a count, so the game is identical at four hundred dogs and at four thousand: the apocalypse comes at 15% of whatever was adopted. Dogs nobody adopts are never born at all.

The ninety days

How the ninety days work

01

A dog you keep does not sit still.

Two clocks run on every living dog. Once a week a handful of them are handed one more trait, and only rare ones: two in every thousand living dogs a week, about a tenth of the pack a year. Every day, two in every ten thousand living dogs die of old age and become angels, about seven in a hundred a year. Nobody chooses either one and nothing you do changes your odds. Holding is the only way your dog gets rarer, and the only way it dies with your name on it.

02

Selling your dog kills it.

Not as a penalty. As the mechanism. For the whole of the first ninety days, the moment a living dog leaves your wallet the contract turns it into a zombie in the same transaction, and the buyer receives the corpse. There is no button, no confirmation and no way to sell a dog and keep it alive. The turn is signed on the collar forever, your handle still on it, and you have joined the horde's side of the count: one of the fifteen in a hundred that ends it for everyone still holding. It is what makes a listing honest: a listed dog is a public announcement that its owner is about to kill it.

03

At 15% first sales, the apocalypse.

The count is first sales only. A dog can turn once and only once, on the day it first leaves the wallet that adopted it, and after that it is a zombie that trades as often as anyone likes without ever moving the number again. So the count is not volume and it cannot be manufactured: it is how many separate dogs have been given up, out of however many were adopted. The line is fixed the moment the door shuts, printed, and never moved again. If 15% of the pack has been given up at any point inside the ninety days, the horde takes every living dog on the next midnight and they come back as zombies: still yours, tradable, never alive again. Fifteen percent over ninety days works out to one first sale per thousand dogs roughly every fourteen hours, sustained the whole way, which is an ordinary amount of churn for a new collection and is exactly why the line is set there. Angels are already gone and cannot be taken.

04

Survive the ninety days and the zombies lose forever.

If the last midnight passes with the count under the line, no dog is ever turned into a zombie by a sale again, and every dog still in its adopting wallet is a living animal that nobody could have bought on any of those ninety days. A living dog still cannot be traded and stay living, so from here letting go makes a vampire, or one time in ten an immortal. That is all that happens, and it is the good ending.

The tempter

I will buy your dog.
Dead.

A tribe that is never tempted has not been tested, and at fifty dollars a dog nobody would ever be. So for the whole ninety days there is a standing public bid, from my wallet, for any living dog in the pack, well above what you paid, paid the moment you accept.

01

The purse is public.

The bid runs from a purse, and from the day the door shuts the wallet and its balance are public next to the count. When it is empty the tempting stops. The purse is sized so that I can start the apocalypse and cannot finish it alone; the rest is up to the pack.

02

Every dog I buy arrives dead.

The contract does not know who the buyer is. A living dog sold to me turns in the same transaction like any other, so the horde lives in my kennel, each corpse with its seller's handle still on the collar, and each one counts toward the fifteen in a hundred that ends it for everyone still holding.

03

I never touch the brush. I do hold the money.

The painters paint, the judge judges, and I am not allowed near either. This is the one place I am in the game, and it is on the wrong side, on purpose. The question the piece asks is whether people who were vouched for will hold an animal in public when somebody offers them ten times what they paid to let it die. Somebody has to make the offer. It should be the person who made the dogs.
