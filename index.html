ALL DOGS™

1985 yellow bandana over blond side hair, blond horseshoe moustache, torn yellow tank top with red patches, bare arms flexed upward

1,000 individual paintings · one zombie apocalypse · one per person

ALL DOGS is the second half of the record of a childhood that has no record. The first half, HOLY, is that adolescence described from memory to a machine. This half is the same childhood in the other hand, composed dog by dog and painted in code, then played as a ninety-day performance by friends of friends: one dog each, every move signed on the collar. The artwork is whatever is left when the ninety days end. The artist draws the dogs; the owners write the story.

Why dogs

The record of a childhood
that has no record.

Nobody has a picture.

My other collection, HOLY, is an evangelical adolescence between 1975 and 1995: church buses, revival carpet, motel pools, baptisms, hard flash. My mom's photo albums were what she prized most. They were lost in hasty moves and storage units nobody kept paying for, and by the time I was ready to go back for them, and I mean ready in the way you have to be ready for something like that, there was nothing left to restore. Nobody has a picture of those years. Nobody photographs the part you remember anyway.

So I am making the record anyway.

ALL DOGS is the same childhood in the other hand. The painter is a program I wrote to paint the way a kid paints: a fat outline, paint running out mid-stroke, an eye a little wrong. These are the drawings that would have been on the fridge, made now by a machine I spend my afternoons teaching.

A record is not a person. The albums would not have brought anyone back either. You make the record anyway. I can paint the dog. I cannot paint who held on to it. The collar records that part: your handle from the first day, and whether you kept the dog or sold it, there for good.

The medium

The paint is code.

In code, I've been building a brush. Each bristle holds a finite load, tests the surface under it, picks up what's still wet, and lays down what contact allows. Long strokes run out. The picture is that surface, lit from one side. Each dog's composition is written into the painter: its face, clothes, pose, and the world around it. The code places the marks; the brush gives them their edges. A seed lets the painter make that dog again at a different resolution.

1939 · Frida, painted

Thirty seconds of the brush at work. Bare cotton first. The ground goes on with the same bristles, twenty-seven strokes. Then every mark the seed calls for, in the order the painter made them. No frame is a dissolve: each one is a photograph of the wet surface after the next stroke landed. Tap it for the sound.

A finite load

A bristle that touches wet paint takes some of it into its tip. Drag white through wet grey and the stroke goes grey for two or three brush widths, then the clean colour in the reservoir comes back. A long stroke starves, and its last third breaks into streaks.

Lit from one side

Every mark adds height and a wet film to a canvas with tooth. Slopes shade. Wet paint catches a highlight. The weave shows through where the paint is thin. Every edge is where the paint stopped. What a given screen makes of that field is a question for that screen, which is the same bargain every painting has always had with the light in the room.

The method · whose art this is

I stayed in the room.

I wrote a constitution for this collection: what a dog is here, what cuteness owes the viewer, how paint should behave, which painters set the bar and why none of them may be copied, the color law that separates the living from the dead. Every rule the machines obey, I authored.

The earlier gauntlet helped build the painter. Five AI painters proposed changes to the code; a critic compared their sheets without being told which was the champion. The scores made a first cut. They did not make the final decision.

Now I also work on dogs one at a time with AI coding tools: choosing the subject, asking for a different body or background, changing the colour, looking again. I work on each picture until I want to keep it. The code is how the picture gets painted.

I have rejected rounds the critic scored highest. I have stopped whole lines of dogs and started others. A good score does not make me want a picture. I have to want it.

The brush still surprises me. A stroke runs dry, a line catches the tooth, two colours meet in a way I want to keep. I can direct the painting and still discover something in it.

In the performance, what a dog is freezes at the reveal: its type, its coat, its wardrobe, its one of one. How it is painted can keep developing for the same ninety days the holding is fought over, and both freeze together at the last midnight. The painter kept that night is the painter forever.

The game

The proposed rules. No contract is deployed yet.

Zombie apocalypse

Dog people, each let in by somebody already here, adopt one dog each and keep it for ninety days. A dog you keep grows older every day and rarer every week. Selling it kills it: the moment a living dog changes hands, the contract turns it into a zombie. If 15% of the pack is sold before the ninety days are up, every living dog turns that same night.

1

Start by adopting your dog. One dog per confirmed handle. Your handle goes on the collar.

2

Then decide: keep it, or kill it. Selling during the ninety days turns it. Whoever takes it receives a corpse, and your sale counts toward the 15% that ends it for everyone else.

If under 15% are sold

The zombies lose, permanently, and the dogs live: the only living dogs that will ever exist are the ones nobody sold.

If 15% are sold

Fifteen in a hundred was enough. Everyone else loses: the apocalypse turns every living dog into a zombie the same night.

The attributes

Two fates.
One collar.

Every dog starts alive. Keep it, and it stays yours. Sell it during the ninety days, and it turns. The owners decide which; the painting has no say in it and neither does the artist.

Faithful

Still alive, still yours. A faithful dog is one that has never moved: not sold, not gifted, not shuffled to another wallet. It is also the only fate that keeps changing. Every week a few living dogs are handed one more rare trait. Every day, two in every ten thousand of them die of old age. Keeping is not standing still.

Zombie

Sold, while the ninety days were still running. Its owner let it go, the sale turned it in the same transaction, and that is signed on the collar forever. Zombies move freely from then on and never change again. A dog turns on its first sale and can never turn twice, so the horde is a count of separate dogs given up, and fifteen in every hundred adopted ends the world for everyone else.

turned · sold
Lived

Every collar carries a number: how many days the dog lived. A zombie is stamped the day it turned. A dog that dies of old age is stamped the day it passed, and that number goes up on the plaque too. The faithful just keep counting, long after the ninety days end. Day 3 (turned before its first walk) and day 3,650 (ten years old) are both rare; the middle belongs to everyone.

Wardrobe

The face, pose, clothes, and place are chosen together for each painting. Something held is something the dog does: it sips the martini, hugs the teddy. A crown belongs in a particular picture. It is not a promised percentage of the edition. Every dog gets this attention, whether it is somebody you recognise or somebody new.

On the piece

Your handle on the collar. The fate. The days lived. The wardrobe it was born with and whatever the weekly drop added to it. The plaque, if it earned one. The drawing itself, one of one from its seed.

All 1,000 dogs will be individual paintings. People, famous dogs, scenes from movies, and dogs with stories of their own. What happens after adoption belongs to the owners: the days lived, the weekly chance of a new trait, and the decision to keep or sell.

The ninety days

How the ninety days work

01

A dog you keep does not sit still.

Two clocks run on every living dog. Once a week a handful of them are handed one more trait, and only rare ones: two in every thousand living dogs a week, about a tenth of the pack a year. Every day, two in every ten thousand living dogs die of old age, about seven in a hundred a year. Nobody chooses either one and nothing you do changes your odds. Holding is the only way your dog gets rarer, and the only way it dies with your name on it.

02

Selling your dog kills it.

Not as a penalty. As the mechanism. For the whole ninety days, the moment a living dog leaves your wallet the contract turns it into a zombie in the same transaction, and whoever takes it receives the corpse. There is no button, no confirmation and no way to sell a dog and keep it alive. The turn is signed on the collar forever, your handle still on it. Offering the dog is a public announcement that its owner is about to kill it.

03

At 15% first sales, the apocalypse.

The count is first sales only. A dog turns once, on the day it first leaves the wallet that adopted it, and moves as often as anyone likes afterward without moving the number again. A gift or a move to your own second wallet counts the same as a sale. So the count cannot be manufactured: it is how many separate dogs have been given up, out of however many were adopted. The line is fixed the moment the door shuts and never moved. If 15% of the pack has been given up at any point inside the ninety days, the horde takes every living dog at the next midnight: still yours, free to move, never alive again. Fifteen percent over ninety days is one first sale per thousand dogs roughly every fourteen hours, which is exactly why the line is set there. Dogs that already died of old age cannot be taken.

04

Survive the ninety days and the zombies lose forever.

If the last midnight passes with the count under the line, no sale can ever turn a dog again, and every dog still in its adopting wallet is a living animal that nobody could have taken on any of those ninety days. That is the good ending.

The drop

Nobody gets in
who wasn't let in.

1,000 dogs, each an individual painting. Invitations begin with people who already collect my work and pass through friends of friends for seven days. One dog per confirmed handle.

01

It starts with my collectors.

The first invitations go to people who already hold something of mine. That first wave is the part of the pack I choose.

02

Adopt, and you get three invites.

Three Twitter handles, named by you. When an invited person adopts, they get three invitations of their own. An invitation does not add another dog to the edition: the limit stays 1,000.

03

Seven days. One thousand paintings.

The invitation window is seven days. There is no separate public sale. The allocation terms will be published before adoption opens, including what happens if more than 1,000 people want a dog.

So the pack is a chain and not a crowd. The ninety days are a test of whether a group of people can hold together, and how the group was assembled is part of the experiment. A scramble for a public list gathers strangers. This gathers a tribe: dog people, friends of friends, every one of them one link from somebody who vouched for them.
