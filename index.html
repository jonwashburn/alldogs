Why dogs

The record of a childhood
that has no record.

Nobody has a picture.

My other collection, HOLY, is an evangelical adolescence between 1975 and 1995: church buses, revival carpet, motel pools, baptisms, hard flash. My mom's photo albums were what she prized most. They were lost in hasty moves and storage units nobody kept paying for, and by the time I was ready to go back for them, and I mean ready in the way you have to be ready for something like that, there was nothing left to restore. Nobody has a picture of those years. Nobody photographs the part you remember anyway.

So I am making the record anyway.

ALL DOGS is the same childhood in the other hand. The painter is a program I wrote to paint the way a kid paints: a fat outline, paint running out mid-stroke, an eye a little wrong. These are the drawings that would have been on the fridge, made now by a machine I spend my afternoons teaching.

A record is not a person. The albums would not have brought anyone back either. You make the record anyway. I can paint the dog. I cannot paint who held on to it. The collar records that part: your handle from the first day, and whether you kept the dog or sold it, there for good.

The medium

The paint is code.

In code, I've been building a brush. Each bristle holds a finite load, tests the surface under it, picks up what's still wet, and lays down what contact allows. Long strokes run out. The picture is that surface, lit from one side. Each dog's composition is written into the painter: its face, clothes, pose, and the world around it. The code places the marks; the brush gives them their edges. A seed lets the painter make that dog again at a different resolution.

1939 · Frida, painted

Thirty seconds of the brush at work. Bare cotton first. The ground goes on with the same bristles, twenty-seven strokes. Then every mark the seed calls for, in the order the painter made them. No frame is a dissolve: each one is a photograph of the wet surface after the next stroke landed.

A finite load.

A bristle that touches wet paint takes some of it into its tip. Drag white through wet grey and the stroke goes grey for two or three brush widths, then the clean colour in the reservoir comes back. A long stroke starves, and its last third breaks into streaks.

Lit from one side.

Every mark adds height and a wet film to a canvas with tooth. Slopes shade. Wet paint catches a highlight. The weave shows through where the paint is thin. Every edge is where the paint stopped.

A living collection

The dog is yours.
The story isn't over.

Adoption is the beginning, not a sale that hurts the dog. Every newly adopted dog starts living. After that, keeping, selling, infection and time leave a permanent mark.

01

A sale changes everything.

A living dog's first resale turns it into a zombie, unless it fights back. One in twenty resists that first sale and becomes an immortal hero instead.

02

One loss can spread.

Each new zombie has a 50% chance to infect one other living dog. Each newly infected dog gets the same chance, and the chain continues. That is 0.5 additional infections per new zombie on average, not half a dog.

03

Sometimes a dog saves another.

Each immortal rescues one zombie that was turned by infection, returning it to living. A dog turned by a sale cannot be rescued. Heroes receive one of five special powers and a new background.

These are the current collection rules in development, not a deployed game. The five powers and final transfer, timing and randomness details will be published before paid adoption. There is no ninety-day finish line or fixed apocalypse threshold.

All dogs go to heaven

Even a dog you keep
can't stay forever.

Every eligible living dog has a 1 in 1,600 chance of natural death each day. That is 0.0625%. When it happens, the dog becomes an angel, with its lifespan and cause of transition preserved.

Some lives are short. Some run for years. The collar remembers who was there.

The approved daily rate gives a mean natural-death waiting time of about 4.38 years while continuously eligible. Sale or infection can end a living episode sooner.

The method · whose art this is

I stayed in the room.

I wrote a constitution for this collection: what a dog is here, what cuteness owes the viewer, how paint should behave, which painters set the bar and why none of them may be copied, the color law that separates the living from the dead. Every rule the machines obey, I authored.

The earlier gauntlet helped build the painter. Five AI painters proposed changes to the code; a critic compared their sheets without being told which was the champion. The scores made a first cut. They did not make the final decision.

Now I also work on dogs one at a time with AI coding tools: choosing the subject, asking for a different body or background, changing the colour, looking again. I work on each picture until I want to keep it. The code is how the picture gets painted.

I have rejected rounds the critic scored highest. I have stopped whole lines of dogs and started others. A good score does not make me want a picture. I have to want it.

The brush still surprises me. A stroke runs dry, a line catches the tooth, two colours meet in a way I want to keep. I can direct the painting and still discover something in it.

The artwork starts as a painting and becomes a record of a relationship. A name, a person, a life lived, and the moments that changed it. The code can record those things. It cannot decide whether you care.
